Curated View Concept and Categorization Logic

Curated View is intended to take a single news event and present it as a clustered story, rather than treating every outlet’s version of that event as a separate headline. The purpose is to help the user quickly understand what happened, what facts are shared across coverage, and how different sources are reporting, confirming, amplifying, or framing the same event.

In plain English, Curated View should give the user the story first, the facts underneath it, and the source trail behind it.

The process begins by pulling in source material about the same event. That source material may include traditional news articles, wire reports, official statements, social media posts, Dataminr alerts, videos, opinion pieces, podcasts, transcripts, press releases, livestreams, or other relevant open-source material. These items are grouped into one shared story cluster so the user can see the broader coverage environment around the event in one place.

Curated View then uses AI analysis to identify the core facts that appear across the clustered material. These facts include who said what, what happened, where it happened, when it happened, what claims were made, what caveats were included, what remains disputed, and what facts are supported by more than one source. The system should distinguish between confirmed facts, attributed claims, official statements, early alerts, opinion framing, and unverified or developing information.

Instead of relying on one outlet’s headline, Curated View generates a neutral story line at the top. The story line should summarize the central event without adopting the political, regional, ownership, state-aligned, or editorial framing of any one source. It should be written plainly and factually, so the user can understand the issue before seeing how different sources shaped or emphasized it.

Under the story line, Curated View provides short, plain-English facts that capture the key points from the clustered coverage. These facts should reflect the common factual core of the story, not the spin of a particular outlet, account, government, or media ecosystem. Where a point comes from only one source, or is based on an official claim, the wording should make that clear.

The detailed view then shows the individual items that contributed to the cluster. Each item should remain visible and traceable by source type, outlet or account, headline or post text, summary, location when available, timing, hyperlink, factuality or reliability indicator when applicable, ownership or control category, and any relevant source-alignment or information-interest context. This allows the user to move from the neutral story line back to the original source material.

The detailed view should not be limited to news media. A social media post may provide the first public claim. A Dataminr alert may indicate early detection. A video may provide visual evidence. An official statement may provide attribution. A wire report may provide baseline reporting. A news article may provide additional sourcing or context. An opinion piece, podcast, or commentary item may show how the story is being interpreted or framed for a specific audience. All of these can contribute to the cluster, but they should be clearly categorized by type so the user understands the role each item plays.

In plain English, the detailed view should show what fed into the story and what kind of source each item is.

The coverage details section should give the user a quick snapshot of the full source environment around the story. It should show how many total items are contributing to the cluster, how many are traditional news sources, how many are social media or open-source items, how many are official or institutional sources, how many are opinion or commentary items, when the cluster was last updated, and whether the source pool is weighted toward a specific outlet type, ownership or control structure, country, region, or narrative environment.

Rather than focusing only on left, right, or center political lean, the coverage details should help the user understand who is contributing to the story, where the information originated, which sources are amplifying it, and whether the cluster is being shaped primarily by independent reporting, official government messaging, state-run media, proxy-linked outlets, social media circulation, opinion commentary, or multimedia evidence. Political leaning can still be included where useful, but it should not be the dominant way bias is understood.

The Ownership / Bias section is intended to show who controls, funds, directs, or meaningfully influences the sources contributing to a story. This matters because ownership and control can shape editorial priorities, source selection, framing, amplification patterns, and the way specific countries, governments, military actions, alliances, institutions, or social movements are portrayed.

Curated View should treat bias as more than left-right political lean. It should assess bias through the lens of source control, institutional alignment, editorial dependence, state interest, and likely information objectives. This is especially important for state-run, state-funded, government-aligned, party-controlled, military-linked, sanctioned, or proxy-amplified media sources that may lack objectivity toward the United States, Western governments, democratic institutions, NATO, Israel, Ukraine, Taiwan, sanctions, military operations, or Western-led international norms.

In plain English, this section should answer: who is behind the source, what interests may shape its coverage, and how does that affect the way the story is presented?

The Ownership / Bias section should make clear whether coverage is coming from independent outlets, corporate media, media conglomerates, private owners, government-funded outlets, state-run media, party-controlled media, military-linked sources, sanctioned entities, or outlets connected to broader proxy or influence networks. For state-run or state-aligned sources, the goal is not simply to label the outlet as biased. The goal is to help the user understand the source’s likely information role.

A source may be promoting a government position, reinforcing an adversary narrative, laundering claims through regional partners, amplifying proxy messaging, undermining confidence in U.S. or Western policy, or shaping perceptions around conflict, sanctions, military activity, diplomacy, legitimacy, sovereignty, civilian harm, or institutional credibility. Curated View should help the user understand not only what the source reported, but why that source may be emphasizing certain facts, omitting others, or framing the story in a particular way.

This section is not meant to decide whether a story is true or false. It is meant to show the control environment behind the coverage and the potential interests shaping the reporting. By making ownership, state alignment, proxy relationships, and institutional bias visible, Curated View helps the user assess how different sources are positioning the same event and what broader information dynamics may be at play.

The factuality or reliability section is intended to show the general reliability profile of the sources contributing to the cluster. For traditional media outlets, this may include publication-level ratings based on sourcing practices, corrections, contextual language, and factual reporting history. A higher factuality rating should suggest that the outlet generally has stronger reporting practices. A mixed or lower rating should signal that the user should apply more caution when relying on that source.

For non-news items, such as social media posts, videos, Dataminr alerts, official statements, or podcasts, the system should avoid implying that a publication-level factuality score applies in the same way. Instead, it should identify the source type and explain the item’s role in the cluster. A social media post may be an initial claim. A video may be visual evidence. An official statement may be attribution. A podcast may be commentary. A Dataminr alert may be early detection. A news article may be secondary reporting. The reliability indicator should help the user understand how much weight to give each item.

In plain English, factuality should not tell the user what to believe. It should tell the user how much caution to apply.

The source geography section is intended to show where the source environment is coming from, while keeping that separate from where the story itself is happening. Curated View should not assume that the country or region of the outlet is the same as the country or region of the story. A U.S. domestic story covered by Russian state media is still a U.S. domestic story, but the outlet origin and ownership or control country matter because they help explain how the story is being framed, why it may be amplified, and what audience or narrative target it may serve.

Source geography should help the user understand whether the story is being shaped primarily by U.S. outlets, regional outlets, international wires, government-linked sources, state-run media, local media close to the event, or a broader proxy or influence ecosystem. This is especially important when state-run or state-aligned outlets are amplifying stories about the United States, Western institutions, NATO, Israel, Ukraine, Taiwan, sanctions, military activity, or democratic governance.

Curated View should organize every story through a layered categorization system. The major categorization fields should include scope, category, region, country or countries, source origin, ownership or control, key entities, and narrative target when relevant. These fields should work together rather than compete with one another. The goal is to make each story easy to sort, filter, compare, and analyze without forcing every item into an overly narrow or misleading category.

Within the user interface, these categorization fields can appear as labels. In other words, categorization is the logic behind how the system understands and organizes the story, while labels are the visible tags or fields the user sees in the product. A user may see labels such as Scope: Global / International, Category: Military Operations and Conflict, Region: Middle East, Topic Country: Iran, Outlet Origin: Russia, or Ownership / Control: State-run Media. Those labels should be understood as the UI expression of the deeper categorization system.

Scope is the broadest organizing layer. It answers whether the story is primarily U.S. domestic or global / international. U.S. Domestic should be used when the story is mainly about internal U.S. politics, law, public opinion, governance, institutions, homeland security, domestic protests, elections, courts, Congress, or federal agencies, and does not primarily concern external actors or international effects. Global / International should be used when the story involves foreign governments, international organizations, overseas conflict, U.S. foreign policy, U.S. military activity abroad, alliances, adversary messaging, sanctions, global markets, maritime security, migration, transnational threats, or any story whose primary meaning extends beyond internal U.S. affairs.

Scope should not replace the topic category. It should streamline the category by telling the user which broad environment the story belongs to. In the UI, scope may appear as a simple label, but behind the label it functions as the top-level categorization modifier.

Category is the main subject field. It answers what kind of story this is. The category should describe the central issue, not the outlet’s framing. It should be based on the substance of the story, such as defense policy, military operations, diplomacy, sanctions, terrorism, information influence, cyber, humanitarian impacts, international law, maritime security, foreign domestic politics, or public governance. A story should usually have one primary category, with secondary categories added only when they are analytically important.

For example, if the story is about U.S. force posture, readiness, weapons programs, or defense leadership, the category may be U.S. Defense Policy and Military Readiness. If the story is about airstrikes, troop movements, maritime operations, ceasefires, or battlefield developments, the category may be Military Operations and Conflict. If the story is about official U.S. engagement with foreign governments, sanctions diplomacy, ceasefire talks, aid packages, or embassy activity, the category may be U.S. Foreign Policy and Diplomacy. If the story is about state media narratives, propaganda, influence activity, public messaging, or adversary framing, the category may be Information Environment and Influence.

Region is the geographic operating environment. It answers what part of the world the story primarily affects. Region should be used when the story has a clear geographic focus beyond one country or when location matters to understanding the story. Regions can include Middle East, Europe, Indo-Pacific, Latin America, Africa, Arctic, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Korean Peninsula, South China Sea, or Global. The region should reflect the story’s main area of impact, not simply the location of the outlet publishing or amplifying the item.

A story about Iran, Israel, Lebanon, or SoH maritime security would generally fall under Middle East. A story about Taiwan, China, the Philippines, or South China Sea activity would generally fall under Indo-Pacific. A story about NATO, Russia, Ukraine, or European defense posture would generally fall under Europe. A story about global energy prices, cyber norms, migration flows, or United Nations action may be categorized as Global if no single region dominates.

Country fields should identify the country or countries central to the story, but Curated View should distinguish between different country roles. Topic Country means the country the story is mainly about. Actor Country means the country taking the action, making the statement, applying pressure, conducting the operation, or driving the policy. Affected Country means the country being targeted, affected, criticized, sanctioned, attacked, defended, or pressured. Event Country means the country where the event physically occurred. Outlet Origin Country means the country where the outlet, account, or source is based, headquartered, registered, or editorially centered. Ownership / Control Country means the country, government, party, owner, corporation, ministry, or institutional network that controls or meaningfully influences the source. Narrative Target means the country, alliance, institution, or population the framing appears aimed at influencing.

In plain English, the topic country tells the user what the story is about. The actor country tells the user who is taking action. The affected country tells the user who is being pressured or impacted. The event country tells the user where the event occurred. The outlet origin country tells the user where the coverage came from. The ownership / control country tells the user who may influence the source. The narrative target tells the user who the framing may be designed to affect.

These country fields may appear in the UI as labels, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. Each one represents a different part of the categorization logic. A story can be about the United States, reported by a Russian outlet, controlled by the Russian state, and aimed at influencing Western perceptions. Those are different categorizations and should remain separate in the UI.

Entity tags should identify the major actors in the story and connect them to the country, region, source, and ownership fields. An entity can be a state actor, non-state actor, institution, alliance, outlet, owner, company, military organization, proxy network, or sanctioned organization. Government entities may include the White House, State Department, Department of War, Treasury, Kremlin, Chinese Foreign Ministry, Iranian Foreign Ministry, or other ministries and agencies. Military entities may include U.S. Central Command, IDF, IRGC, PLA, Russian Ministry of Defense, NATO military bodies, or other armed forces. Alliance or multilateral entities may include NATO, EU, UN, G7, ASEAN, Arab League, or other international organizations. Proxy or non-state entities may include Hezbollah, Houthis, Hamas, militias, insurgent groups, armed political movements, or terrorist organizations. Media entities may include RT, Sputnik, Xinhua, Global Times, Press TV, Al Mayadeen, Telesur, KCNA, or other state-run, state-funded, party-controlled, government-aligned, or proxy-amplifying outlets. Ownership entities may include government ministries, ruling parties, media conglomerates, private owners, telecom companies, sanctioned entities, or known influence networks.

Entity tags are also part of the categorization system. In the UI, they may appear as labels attached to the story or source item, but their purpose is to show which actors are involved and how they connect to the broader story environment.

The overall categorization logic should begin by classifying the story according to what happened. The system should then assign the broad scope, identify the primary category, assign a region when the story has a meaningful geographic focus, identify the relevant country roles, separate source origin and ownership / control from story geography, assign entity tags, and identify the narrative target when the source environment or framing makes that analytically useful.

In plain English, categorize the story by the event, categorize the source by who produced or controls it, and categorize the narrative environment by who the framing appears designed to influence. The labels shown in the UI should make those distinctions visible without collapsing them into one generic country, region, or bias field.

This prevents Curated View from miscategorizing a story just because it is covered by a foreign outlet. It also lets the user see when certain countries, state media systems, or proxy networks are disproportionately amplifying a story about the United States, the West, or a specific conflict.

For example, if Russian state media covers U.S. domestic protests, the story should remain categorized as U.S. Domestic because the event is about internal U.S. affairs. The region would be North America, the topic and event country would be the United States, the outlet origin country would be Russia, and the ownership / control country would also likely be Russia if the outlet is state controlled. The narrative target may include U.S. audiences, Western institutions, or confidence in U.S. governance. In the UI, those distinctions could appear as separate labels, but the underlying categorization should keep the event, source, ownership, and narrative target separate.

If Chinese state media covers NATO activity, the story should be categorized as Global / International because it concerns alliance and security dynamics. The region would likely be Europe, the topic entity would be NATO, the actor countries would include the United States and NATO members as applicable, the outlet origin country would be China, and the ownership / control country would likely be China if the outlet is state controlled. The narrative target may include NATO, the United States, Western security architecture, or international audiences skeptical of Western alliances.

If Iranian state media covers Gaza, Israel, or U.S. regional military activity, the story should be categorized based on the actual substance of the event. It may fall under Military Operations and Conflict, Humanitarian / Civilian Protection, U.S. Foreign Policy and Diplomacy, or Information Environment and Influence depending on the content. The region would likely be Middle East. The topic countries may include Israel, Palestinian territories, Iran, Lebanon, Yemen, Syria, or Iraq, as applicable. The outlet origin and ownership / control country may be Iran. The narrative target may include the United States, Israel, Western policy, regional legitimacy, or resistance-axis audiences.

A clean Curated View categorization for a U.S. sanctions story might identify the scope as Global / International, the category as Economic Statecraft and Sanctions, the region as Middle East, the topic country as Iran, the actor country as the United States, and the affected country as Iran. If the cluster includes U.S., Israeli, Iranian, Russian, Chinese, or regional media sources, those outlet origin countries should be shown separately. The ownership / control field should then identify whether the coverage comes from independent, corporate, government-aligned, state-run, proxy-linked, or other source environments. Key entities may include POTUS, the Treasury Department, the Iranian government, sanctioned entities, and contributing media outlets. In the UI, these would appear as separate labels or fields, but they should all flow from the categorization logic.

The final purpose of Curated View is not just to summarize news. It is to give the user a transparent, source-aware view of the story. The user should be able to see the neutral story line, the key shared facts, the source items behind the cluster, the type and reliability of those sources, who owns or controls them, where the story is happening, where the coverage is coming from, which entities are involved, and what broader information dynamics may be shaping the way the story is presented.
