Methodology — MCOP Version 3.0: Curated View
What you're looking at
The Curated view is a single, scannable picture of the defense and national-security news that matters right now. Instead of a raw firehose, it groups coverage into stories and ranks them so the most important items sit at the top.
Top stories and ranking
Stories are ordered by a mix of how important and how fresh they are. As a story ages, it gradually settles down the list, letting newer developments rise. Top stories can show two kinds of items: Single-source items and Clustered stories 
Single-source items are individual media content items like articles, videos, or podcasts that arrive timely, high-priority, or operationally relevant. These are shown as single reports until enough trusted coverage exists to form a story.
Clustered stories are formed when multiple trusted reporting sources cover the same media event. MCOP pulls those reports into one card so you can see the event, the shared facts, and the range of coverage without reading ten near-duplicate headlines.
How a “clustered story" is built
A clustered story forms when the same event is reported by two or more trusted outlets. We pull those reports together into one card so you can see the whole event at a glance rather than ten near-duplicate headlines.
The cluster’s headline and short summary on each card are written to be neutral — a plain description of what happened, drawn from across all the sources that covered it.
Source Types (Contributing vs Attached)
A clustered story shows how individual sources framed the event, so you can see the spread of coverage — not just a single take. Every source on a story is one of two types, which determines how much weight it carries.
Contributing sources are outlets and entities that score consistently across (name 3 places we check) for a rating of high factuality or greater and are known for citing sources, (include criteria) — they are what allow a legitimate story to form in the first place.
Different outlets often describe the same facts in different ways. Sources with mixed or lower factuality, biased ownership, etc… may also contribute to a story but cannot alter facts or form clusters on their own …  etc.
For less-established or state-controlled outlets, the story also estimates how much of that source's coverage lines up with the corroborated reporting, shown as a rough percentage, and points out where it diverges — added spin, one-sided emphasis, or details it leaves out. The percentage is an overall read of the source's framing, not a simple count of points, so a lower number flags coverage that pulls away from what the trusted sources agree on.
Attached sources —can join an existing story to add context, but they can never create one on their own. These may include opinion pieces, social posts, videos, Dataminr alerts, official statements, less-established outlets, or other material that adds context, attribution, early warning, visual evidence, or framing.
Content metadata (Topic · Focus · Time · Source Count)
Just above each story's headline is a single line of small print that packs several facts together, separated by dots — for example:
Military Ops · Indo-Pacific · 2h ago · 4 sources
The first two parts are the story's Topic · Focus: a high-level Topic for what kind of story it is, and a Focus for which part of the world it affects. The rest of the line shows where the story came from — the outlet for a single report, or how many sources back a multi-source story — and when it was published or last updated.
The Topic comes first — for example Military Ops — and tags the story by subject. Topics are drawn from a fixed list of about twenty high-level subjects, so the same kind of story is always labeled the same way and the list stays short enough to scan for what you care about. There is one catch-all, Other / Emerging, kept deliberately small for genuinely new subjects that none of the real buckets fit — the system actively avoids parking a story there when a proper subject bucket applies.
A Topic describes a story's subject, not its importance. A story gets its true subject even if it scored low or wasn't judged strategically relevant — a public-health, natural-disaster, severe-weather, or environmental story is tagged Disaster & Health rather than dropped into the catch-all just because it sits lower in the feed.
How the Topic is set depends on where the story came from. For stories the team submits, a person picks the Topic from the same fixed list. For everything else the AI assigns it from that list, with a safety net that re-routes an over-vague label to the right bucket when the subject clearly matches one. A Topic is set once, and a person's choice is never overwritten.
The Focus follows the Topic — a region such as Indo-Pacific, a country such as Taiwan, or sometimes a two-country pair. It marks where the story's impact lands: what actually happened and where, not where the outlet that reported it is based. The Focus is always worked out automatically from the article's content.
Elsewhere on the page, a Blind spot tag appears when a story the wider press is covering heavily is missing from our feed. It is flagged rather than hidden, so nothing important slips by unnoticed.
